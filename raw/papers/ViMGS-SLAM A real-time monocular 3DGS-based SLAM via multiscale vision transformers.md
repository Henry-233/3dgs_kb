## 1\. Introduction

Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (VSLAM) serves as a fundamental technology for constructing dense metric 3D maps of complex environments, thereby providing the critical geometric foundation for autonomous systems. Among its various implementations, monocular VSLAM presents a compelling yet challenging paradigm due to its reliance on a single camera, which introduces significant difficulties in recovering 3D geometry from 2D images. Accurate depth estimation remains a core challenge, evolving from supervised methods—constrained by expensive annotations—to more scalable self-supervised paradigms.

Self-supervised monocular depth estimation, which bypasses the requirement for expensive ground-truth depth by framing depth prediction as a view synthesis problem, has garnered substantial research interest. This paradigm leverages the principle of photometric consistency, jointly optimizing depth and pose estimation networks to minimize reprojection error across multiple views of a scene. Despite notable progress, self-supervised monocular depth estimation remains fundamentally constrained by several geometric challenges: scale ambiguity, unreliable depth cues in textureless regions, and blurred object boundaries resulting from insufficient geometric priors. To address these limitations, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a promising direction, offering global self-attention mechanisms that more effectively model long-range dependencies and integrate contextual information compared to conventional convolutional networks. Variants tailored for dense prediction tasks, such as Multi-scale Vision Transformers (MViT) and shifted-window architectures, aim to better capture hierarchical features and fine-grained local details. These architectural innovations are theoretically valuable for geometric reasoning, as they can yield more robust feature representations in textureless regions and improve handling of occlusions through global context. Nevertheless, within the self-supervised learning paradigm, these advanced architectures continue to grapple with fundamental theoretical limitations. Despite their enhanced representational capacity, they still struggle to resolve inherent scale ambiguity and remain limited in capturing geometric structures in textureless or occluded regions. Consequently, while ViTs enhance representational capacity, their potential for achieving robust, metric-aware geometric understanding remains constrained under the self-supervised paradigm.

Concurrently, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a powerful explicit scene representation method, offering new possibilities for novel view synthesis and scene reconstruction. Its explicit and differentiable nature enables high-fidelity, real-time rendering, making it particularly attractive for VSLAM systems that require both mapping and visualization. As demonstrated in, 3DGS-based methods consistently outperform NeRF-based approaches in rendering quality, achieving higher PSNR and SSIM scores while maintaining lower LPIPS, which highlights the superiority of explicit scene representations for photorealistic reconstruction in SLAM systems. Integrating 3DGS into monocular VSLAM introduces new demands and opportunities for the front-end perception module. Notably, among the 3DGS-based methods listed in Tables??, only MonoGS supports monocular input, yet its performance still leaves room for improvement, motivating our work to enhance monocular capability. The lack of reliable depth cues leads to significant scale ambiguity during Gaussian initialization, while sparse observational geometry often results in incomplete reconstruction and accumulated pose drift. To tackle these issues, several innovative strategies have emerged: hybrid architectures that combine traditional direct visual odometry with Gaussian mapping, photometric confidence-aware Gaussian management, and multi-scale deep regularization to mitigate scale drift. Specifically, accurate initialization and continuous optimization of the 3D Gaussian map necessitate robust geometric priors, which is precisely the kind of global, context-aware understanding that advanced ViT architectures are designed to provide. The theoretical synergy is compelling, as ViTs can deliver strong, globally-informed features to estimate stable depth and infer scene structure from a single view, thereby guiding the 3DGS representation towards metric consistency and mitigating scale drift. Conversely, 3DGS provides a concrete, optimizable 3D structure where these learned features can be grounded and refined through rendering-based losses. However, effectively unifying these two paradigms within a monocular SLAM system presents significant challenges. These include designing an architecture that seamlessly bridges the 2D hierarchical feature space of a ViT with the 3D volumetric representation of Gaussian splats, managing the computational complexity of both components to maintain real-time performance, and devising a joint optimization strategy that leverages both the photometric fidelity of 3DGS rendering and the robustness of ViTs to visual ambiguities. Addressing these challenges is crucial for advancing monocular dense SLAM beyond its current limitations.

To address these challenges, we present ViMGS-SLAM, a novel monocular VSLAM framework that tightly integrates a Multi-scale Vision Transformer (MViT) with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). The MViT acts as a geometric perception backbone, producing metric-scale depth priors through a hierarchical pyramid architecture that captures both fine local details and global context. These depth priors initialize and constrain an explicit 3D Gaussian scene representation, which is then continuously optimized via a synchronous tracking–mapping pipeline. The tracking module leverages photometric and depth residuals for accurate camera pose estimation, while the mapping module refines Gaussian parameters under anisotropic regularization to preserve sharp boundaries and suppress artifacts. An adaptive keyframe selection mechanism balances reconstruction quality and computational efficiency, and a newly introduced adaptive interval adjustment further enhances robustness to scene complexity. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section reviews related work on monocular depth estimation, self-supervised depth learning, and 3DGS-based SLAM. Section details the proposed framework, including the MViT architecture, the 3DGS representation, and the tracking, keyframing, and mapping modules. Section presents extensive experimental results on depth estimation, novel-view rendering, 3D Gaussian reconstruction, trajectory accuracy, ablation studies, and runtime analysis. Section concludes the paper and discusses limitations and future directions.

## 2\. Related work

### 2.1. Monocular depth estimation

Based on deep learning, monocular depth estimation methods can be principally categorized into supervised, semi-supervised, and self-supervised paradigms. 1) Supervised methods establish direct image-to-depth mappings using pixel-wise ground truth annotations. Representative works include DCNNs and VGG-based frameworks, which achieve high accuracy but require expensive depth annotations, limiting their scalability in real-world applications. 2) Semi-supervised methods mitigate data constraints through consistency regularization and pseudo-labeling techniques. While reducing annotation dependency, these approaches remain susceptible to error propagation and domain shift between labeled and unlabeled data distributions,. 3) Self-supervised methods have gained prominence by reformulating depth estimation as a view synthesis problem. These approaches leverage geometric constraints from stereo or monocular sequences, minimizing photometric discrepancies through image warping operations. Recent advances include Dyna-MSDepth, which employs multi-scale spatial-semantic interactions to generate stable depth estimates in dynamic environments. Despite progress, self-supervised monocular depth estimation faces persistent challenges: (1) dynamic object motion violating static scene assumptions, (2) inherent scale ambiguity in monocular setups, and (3) escalating training complexity with multi-modal data. Future research should therefore prioritize the development of efficient multi-scale architectures that achieve robust scale consistency.

### 2.2. Self-supervised depth estimation

Self-supervised depth estimation has garnered substantial research attention due to its label-free training paradigm. Nevertheless, two major challenges still persist. First, self-supervised frameworks inherently lack metric scale recovery, which necessitates auxiliary sensor data (e.g., inertial measurements or camera intrinsics) to establish physical scale Refs.. However, inconsistencies in camera configurations across training datasets hinder the development of unified scale-aware models. While mainstream datasets emphasize structured scenes, they remain limited in representing dynamic objects and extreme illumination variations. Addressing scale invariance and offset robustness across heterogeneous datasets thus constitutes a critical research direction for self-supervised paradigms. Second, domain gaps between datasets significantly degrade model generalizability. Recent advances in zero-shot cross-dataset generalization aim to bridge this gap by enabling metric-compatible predictions without dataset-specific fine-tuning. Pioneering works such as MegaDepth and MiDaS have demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale multi-dataset training, while OmniData introduced systematic strategies for cross-domain adaptation. Emerging architectures leveraging Transformers, ViT and diffusion models exhibit enhanced capacity for cross-dataset feature disentanglement, achieving notable progress in scale-consistent depth transfer. Despite these advancements, limitations persist in three aspects: 1) suboptimal accuracy under occlusions and textureless regions, 2) sensitivity to camera parameter variations, 3) compromised inference efficiency in multi-scale frameworks. To address these challenges, we propose a hierarchical multi-scale ViT architecture that synergistically integrates local texture details and global contextual cues through three distinct resolution pathways.

### 2.3. 3DGS-based SLAM

Within VSLAM research, reconstructing metrically consistent 3D environments from monocular image sequences remains a fundamental challenge. Extensive studies have demonstrated that elliptical primitives outperform conventional point or line features in geometric localization accuracy due to their enhanced spatial constraints,. A seminal advancement was made by Zwicker et al., who introduced Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting - a sophisticated rendering framework employing anisotropic Gaussian kernels. Their work proved that EWA Splatting achieves alias-free reconstruction while preserving sharp discontinuities in volumetric data, effectively handling non-spherical scattering effects. This methodology demonstrates exceptional versatility across regular, curvilinear, and unstructured volumetric datasets, establishing a unified framework for multi-modal data processing. Subsequent ellipse-based methods advanced dynamic scene understanding, yet practical adoption in SLAM remains constrained by reliance on precise calibration, multi-view constraints, and sparse observation sensitivity. The advent of 3DGS has revolutionized this paradigm by enabling unified optimization of geometry and appearance through differentiable volumetric rendering, significantly advancing SLAM reconstruction efficiency and visual quality. Despite these advancements, monocular 3DGS SLAM implementations remain under-explored, particularly regarding scale consistency in depth estimation and computational complexity management. Our work addresses these limitations through three key innovations: 1) A hybrid depth prediction network combining monocular geometric priors with multi-scale Gaussian uncertainty modeling, 2) A ViT-gaussian fusion framework for photorealistic novel view synthesis, and 3) Adaptive algorithms enabling real-time performance on both RGB and RGB-D streams.

## 3\. Methods

### 3.1. System overview

illustrates the architectural framework of the ViMGS-SLAM system, which aims to achieve high-fidelity scene reconstruction and real-time rendering capabilities under complex illumination conditions. The system supports both RGB-D and RGB-only sensor configurations.

For monocular inputs, the MViT module (Section ) generates dense depth priors through multi-scale attention mechanisms, ensuring geometrically consistent depth estimation without compromising temporal coherence. The system supports both RGB-D and RGB-only sensor configurations. Following depth initialization or RGB-D data acquisition, the 3DGS representation module (Section ) transforms input data into anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives via differentiable reparameterization. These primitives explicitly encode scene geometry (position, covariance) and photometric properties, forming a continuous volumetric representation suitable for differentiable rendering. Concurrently, the camera tracking module (Section 3.4.1) estimates camera poses in real time by iteratively minimizing a composite loss function that incorporates both L1 distance and structural similarity (SSIM). This formulation effectively balances subpixel intensity alignment with structural consistency, ensuring stable and accurate pose regression. The tracking output is then processed by the keyframing module (Section 3.4.2), which employs an adaptive selection strategy based on relative pose displacement and keyframe overlap density, complemented by an adaptive interval adjustment mechanism that dynamically modulates insertion frequency according to tracking loss. This strategy systematically prunes redundant keyframes to optimize computational efficiency while preserving the topological integrity of the constructed map. Within the mapping module (Section 3.4.3), ViMGS-SLAM dynamically regulates the integration of 3D Gaussian primitives based on real-time pose estimations from the front-end tracking module, achieving iterative optimization of scene geometry parameters under isotropic regularization to preserve spatial–temporal consistency and suppress artifacts. Through this tightly coupled architecture, ViMGS-SLAM maintains sub-centimeter geometric accuracy and real-time performance, even in challenging scenarios involving textureless surfaces, occlusions, or repeated patterns.

### 3.2. Multi-scale Vision Transformers (MViT)

To enable geometrically accurate 3D reconstruction in visual SLAM and overcome the limitations of self-supervised monocular depth estimation, we propose a novel architecture based on MViT. This design integrates multi-scale visual analysis with geometric constraints in feature learning, addressing key challenges in self-supervised paradigms such as scale ambiguity, sensitivity to textureless regions, and blurred object boundaries.

As shown in, the model comprises two tightly coupled components. First, the multi-scale input embedding stage employs linear projection and scale-adaptive positional encoding to map multi-resolution image patches into a unified feature space. All input images —whether from TUM RGB-D (originally 640 × 480) or other databases—are padded to a square and resized to 640 × 640. The MViT then constructs a three-level pyramid by further resizing to 640 × 640, 320 × 320, and 160 × 160. At each scale, the image is divided into non-overlapping patches using a convolutional layer with kernel size 16 × 16 and stride 16. Each patch is processed by a weight-shared Vision Transformer encoder, producing feature maps of size 24 × 24 per patch. These patch-level features are then merged into a full-resolution feature map at the corresponding pyramid level using a sliding-window merging strategy with 25% overlap, ensuring seamless reconstruction across patch boundaries. Subsequent feature projection employs 1 × 1 convolutions (stride 1) for channel alignment, spatial refinement uses 3 × 3 convolutions (stride 1), and upsampling stages rely on 2 × 2 transposed convolutions (stride 2) to progressively recover resolution. All convolutional layers in the feature fusion blocks and the output head use the ReLU activation function. This efficient multi-scale design enables real-time operation under fast vehicle motion by keeping inference latency low. Second, the multi-scale feature fusion stage implements an attention-based feature pyramid network that ensures consistency between local geometry and global context through cross-scale interaction and spatial–semantic alignment, enhancing depth discontinuity preservation and geometry inference in weakly-textured regions.

The multi-scale feature input and embedding module serves as the core encoding architecture of our MViT. It decomposes the input image into multi-scale patches $\mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ at scales $s \in \mathcal{S}$, applies linear projection via a learnable embedding matrix $\mathbf{E}$ together with scale-aware positional encodings $\mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$, and extracts hierarchical representations using a pre-trained dual-branch Transformer encoder $\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)}$. Each patch is processed by the weight-shared encoder, producing a feature map of size 24 × 24. These patch-level features are subsequently merged and upsampled to construct a multi-scale feature pyramid comprising five levels with spatial resolutions of 320 × 320, 160 × 160, 80 × 80, 40 × 40, and 20 × 20, and channel dimensions of 256, 512, 1024, 1024, and 1024, respectively. Inspired by BeiT, the dual-branch encoder is designed to capture both local and global visual patterns: a local branch focuses on fine-grained details, while a global branch models long-range semantic dependencies. Through the fusion function $\Phi$, which performs cross-scale concatenation and spatial-semantic alignment, these two streams collectively form a unified multi-scale feature pyramid. This design preserves both local geometric structures and global contextual understanding, establishing an essential foundation for accurate geometric reconstruction in subsequent stages. The unified multi-scale feature representation $\mathcal{F}$ can be written as: (1) $\mathcal{F} = \Phi \left(\left(\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)} \left(\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} + \mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}\right)\right)_{s \in \mathcal{S}}\right)$ where the embedded representation for an input image at scale $s$ is given by $\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} + \mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$. The token sequence $\mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ is linearly projected via $\mathbf{E}$ and augmented with scale-dependent trainable positional encodings $\mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$. A Transformer encoder $\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)} \left(\right. \cdot \left.\right)$ with $L$ layers then processes the embedded features, extracting rich contextual representations through multi-head self-attention and multilayer perceptron modules. The fusion function $\Phi \left(\cdot_{s \in \mathcal{S}}\right)$ integrates encoded features across all scales in $\mathcal{S}$ via cross-scale concatenation and spatial–semantic alignment, yielding a unified multi-scale feature representation $\mathcal{F}$.

The dense feature upsampling and depth reconstruction module, built upon a DPT-based decoder, accurately maps multi-scale features to high-resolution depth estimates. After merging and upsampling, the obtained feature pyramid with five levels—320 × 320, 160 × 160, 80 × 80, 40 × 40, and 20 × 20—is first fused with global contextual features and then progressively upsampled via $2 \times$ transposed convolutions, ultimately restoring the original input resolution of 640 × 640. Inter-scale misalignment is mitigated through explicit spatial alignment operations. The aligned features from all scales are then aggregated. The resulting unified multi-scale representation is condensed into a single-channel inverse depth map using a 1 × 1 convolutional layer. This design effectively integrates multi-scale cues in a coarse-to-fine manner, restoring high-resolution geometry while preserving pixel-level geometric continuity and sharp boundaries—forming the core of our sub-pixel geometric fidelity. The predicted inverse depth map $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1}$ can be written as: (2) $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1} = \mathbf{W}_{d} \circ \text{Upsample}_{1 : 1} \left(\right. \underset{s \in \mathcal{S}}{\oplus} \text{Align} \left(\right. \text{Upsample}_{t \leftarrow s} \left(\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} \oplus \mathbf{F}_{g}\right) \left.\right) \left.\right)$ where $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1}$ is the predicted inverse depth map, generated by applying a pointwise convolutional kernel $\mathbf{W}_{d}$ to the output of a final upsampling operation $\text{Upsample}_{1 : 1}$, which restores the aggregated multi-scale features to the original input resolution. This aggregation $\oplus_{s \in \mathcal{S}}$ combines features from all scales $s$ in the set $\mathcal{S}$, where each scale’s contribution is processed by a spatial alignment function $\text{Align}$ that corrects feature misalignments. The input to the alignment function is the upsampled result of $\text{Upsample}_{t \leftarrow s}$, which rescales the fused features from scale $s$ to a common target scale $t$. This fusion $\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} \oplus \mathbf{F}_{g}$ combines scale-specific encoder features $\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ with global contextual features $\mathbf{F}_{g}$ via a skip connection.

The MViT is trained as a zero-shot metric depth estimator on a diverse mixture of real-world and synthetic datasets using a combination of supervised losses, including mean absolute error (MAE) on metric ground truth and scale-and-shift-invariant losses on non-metric data. This training enables the network to directly predict absolute-scale inverse depth maps from a single RGB image without requiring camera intrinsics or metadata, providing a robust metric prior for subsequent SLAM modules.

### 3.3. 3DGS representation

The 3DGS representation provides a unified parametric form for scene modeling that bridges continuous 3D geometry and discrete rendering through a collection of anisotropic Gaussians. The collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \left{\right. G_{i} \left.\right}_{i = 1}^{N}$ is defined as: (3) $\mathcal{G} = \left(G_{i} \left(\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}_{i} , \mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} , O_{i}\right)\right)_{i = 1}^{N} , \mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} = \mathbf{R}_{i} \text{diag} \left(\right. \mathbf{S}_{i} \left.\right) \mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}$ Where $\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the Gaussian’s center, $\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is its covariance matrix, and $O_{i} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the opacity. The covariance is decomposed into a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{i} \in SO \left(\right. 3 \left.\right)$ and scaling factors $\mathbf{S}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, ensuring positive semi-definiteness and enabling stable gradient-based optimization. This parametric form enables efficient differentiable optimization and direct splat-based rasterization.

During rendering, each 3D Gaussian is projected to 2D via a local affine approximation and composited using alpha blending. The projected 2D covariance $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}$ is derived from the Jacobian of the projective transform, while the final pixel color is obtained by depth-ordered blending of all contributing Gaussians based on their projected densities and opacities. (4) $\mathbf{C} \left(\right. p \left.\right) = \underset{i \in \mathcal{N}}{\sum} \mathbf{c}_{i} \alpha_{i} \prod_{j = 1}^{i - 1} \left(\right. 1 - \alpha_{j} \left.\right) , \alpha_{i} = O_{i} exp \left(- \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{\top} \overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}^{- 1} \mathbf{\Delta}_{i}\right)$ Where $\mathbf{\Delta}_{i} = p - \overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}}_{i}$ is the pixel offset, $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}}_{i}$ and $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}$ are the projected mean and covariance, $\alpha_{i}$ is the effective density, and $\mathbf{c}_{i}$ is the Gaussian’s color. This formulation enables real-time, high-quality rendering through differentiable splatting and ordered blending.

### 3.4. Framework

Our framework comprises three tightly integrated components. Tracking estimates camera poses through photometric and depth residuals, keyframing maintains a representative subset of views for efficient mapping, and mapping optimizes the 3D Gaussian representation with geometric constraints.

These modules form a closed-loop system where tracking relies on keyframes for pose initialization, mapping utilizes keyframe observations for scene reconstruction, and keyframe selection is guided by both tracking stability and mapping quality requirements.

(1) Tracking. Camera pose estimation is initialized via orthographic projection and optimized through a combined photometric and depth loss. The hybrid loss leverages both L1 and SSIM metrics for color alignment and incorporates depth residuals when available, with adaptive weighting between modalities. Gradient computation utilizes CUDA-based rasterization for efficiency, while Gaussian parameters remain fixed to ensure mapping stability. The tracking loss is formulated as: (5) $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}} = \lambda_{c} \left(\mathcal{L}_{1} \left(\right. \hat{I} , I \left.\right) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}} \left(\right. \hat{I} , I \left.\right)\right) \bigodot M_{\text{rgb}} + \lambda_{d} \mathcal{L}_{1} \left(\right. \hat{D} , D \left.\right) \bigodot M_{\text{depth}}$ Where $\hat{I}$ and $\hat{D}$ represent the rendered image and depth, $I$ and $D$ are ground-truth measurements, $M_{\text{rgb}}$ and $M_{\text{depth}}$ indicate valid pixels, and $\bigodot$ is element-wise multiplication. In monocular operation, where depth measurements are unavailable, we set $\lambda_{d} = 0$ and rely solely on photometric loss for pose estimation. However, to anchor scene geometry to a metric scale during mapping, the MViT-predicted depth is treated as pseudo ground truth $D$, enforcing geometric consistency through the depth term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$. Optimization runs for $\geq$ 50 iterations per frame using Adam to ensure convergence without map updates.

2) Keyframing. Keyframe management employs a sliding window strategy to balance reconstruction quality and computational load. It consists of two complementary components: a geometry-based selection criterion and an adaptive interval adjustment mechanism. New keyframes are inserted based on overlap and displacement thresholds relative to recent frames, while outdated ones are removed when overlap diminishes. This ensures sufficient viewpoint coverage while maintaining real-time operation. The keyframe selection criteria are defined as: (6) $\text{Insert if}: \text{IoU} \left(\right. \mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n} \left.\right) < \theta \text{or} \frac{L_{m n}}{\hat{D}_{m}} > \xi ,$  $\text{Remove if}: \text{OC} \left(\right. \mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n} \left.\right) < \theta - \xi$ where $\mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n}$ denote Gaussian representations, $\text{IoU}$ and $\text{OC}$ measure geometric overlap, $L_{m n}$ is relative displacement, and $\hat{D}_{m}$ is median depth. Thresholds $\theta , \xi$ are dataset-specific (e.g., TUM: $\theta = 0 . 85 , \xi = 0 . 05$; Replica: $\theta = 0 . 95 , \xi = 0 . 04$). Monocular setups use static thresholds, which may limit adaptability.

To address this limitation, we introduce an adaptive keyframe interval adjustment mechanism that dynamically modulates the insertion frequency based on scene complexity. The keyframe interval $\Delta_{\text{kf}}$ is updated according to the tracking loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}}$ as follows: (7) $\Delta_{\text{kf}} = \begin{bmatrix} max \left(\right. 2 , \Delta_{\text{default}} - 2 \left.\right) , & \text{if} \mathcal{L}_{\text{track}} > \omega \\ min \left(\right. 3 , \Delta_{\text{default}} + 1 \left.\right) , & \text{otherwise} \end{bmatrix}$ where $\Delta_{\text{default}}$ is the default interval and $\omega$ is a predefined loss threshold. The tracking loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}}$ serves as a proxy for scene complexity and motion uncertainty. When the loss exceeds $\omega$, indicating challenging conditions, the interval is reduced (subject to a minimum of 2) to insert keyframes more frequently, thereby strengthening geometric constraints and mitigating primitive distortion. Conversely, when the loss is low, the interval is increased (up to a maximum of 3) to reduce computational redundancy. This adaptive strategy enhances geometric consistency and textural authenticity while maintaining a favorable balance between resource usage and reconstruction quality.

(3) Mapping. Scene reconstruction optimizes Gaussian parameters with explicit regularization to constrain anisotropic scaling in poorly observed regions. The mapping loss combines photometric, depth, and isotropic regularization terms, preventing artifact formation while preserving geometric fidelity under limited viewpoints. The mapping objective is defined as: (8) $\mathcal{L}_{\text{map}} = \lambda_{c} \mathcal{L}_{\text{color}} + \lambda_{d} \mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}} + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \left(\parallel \mathbf{S}_{h} - \overset{̄}{S}_{h} \mathbf{1} \parallel\right)_{2}$ Where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{color}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$ mirror the tracking losses, $\mathbf{S}_{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the Gaussian scaling vector, $\overset{̄}{S}_{h}$ is its mean, and $\mathbf{1}$ is a vector of ones. The regularization term penalizes deviation from isotropic scaling, suppressing excessive elongation. Weights satisfy $\lambda_{c} + \lambda_{d} = 1$ (with $\lambda_{d} = 0$ if depth is unavailable) and $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ controls regularization strength. Based on empirical validation, we set $\lambda_{c} = 0 . 8$, $\lambda_{d} = 0 . 2$ when depth is available, and $\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 0 . 05$.

Specifically, when operating in monocular mode, the depth prior $D$ in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$ is obtained from the MViT forward pass on the current keyframe. The L1 penalty between the rendered depth $\hat{D}$ and this prior anchors the optimization to a metric scale and effectively mitigates scale drift over time.

### 4.7. Runtime results analysis

summarizes the end-to-end runtime performance of ViMGS-SLAM on the TUM fr2/desk sequence. The system achieves an average frame rate of 2.7 FPS when processing monocular RGB streams and 2.3 FPS with RGB-D input. These figures correspond to the complete synchronous execution of the pipeline per frame: tracking, keyframe management, and mapping are performed sequentially, guaranteeing that the map is consistently updated with the most recent observations. The mapping module averages 19.6 optimization iterations per keyframe in monocular mode and 17.7 iterations when depth data is available; this iteration count directly influences the overall frame rate, as each iteration involves gradient computation and parameter updates for the growing set of Gaussian primitives. The observed trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and throughput reflects the inherent cost of dense geometry optimization in a synchronous design, while the rendering stage alone sustains over 1100 FPS, confirming the efficiency of the differentiable Gaussian splatting backend. To better understand the computational characteristics, we examine the rendering subsystem separately. As shown in, the color refinement stage—which involves only forward rendering of the 3D Gaussian representation—achieves an average of 1130.2 FPS, with a peak of 1440.8 FPS. This high throughput demonstrates the exceptional efficiency of the differentiable Gaussian splatting pipeline. However, the overall frame rate remains lower because each tracking step requires multiple rendering passes to compute the photometric loss and gradients, and the mapping step iteratively updates Gaussian parameters. The computational cost of mapping scales with the number of Gaussians, which in our experiments grows to approximately 95,000 primitives on the TUM fr2 sequence. Consequently, the observed trade-off between frame rate and reconstruction accuracy reflects the inherent cost of dense geometry optimization. While the current synchronous design prioritizes geometric consistency, future work could explore asynchronous execution or adaptive iteration budgets to further increase throughput while maintaining quality.

While ViMGS-SLAM achieves accurate dense reconstruction and real-time rendering, its current design does not incorporate explicit loop closure, relocalization after tracking failure, or long-horizon drift correction—modules that are essential for a complete SLAM system. This is not a limitation specific to our approach; indeed, most existing 3DGS-based SLAM methods also focus on front-end tracking and dense mapping, with global optimization and loop detection remaining open research challenges in this emerging paradigm. Consequently, our system behaves as an odometry-only framework, and the absolute trajectory error gradually accumulates over extended sequences, as observed in on the TUM fr2/desk dataset (2965 frames). In challenging scenarios such as rapid motion or severe occlusion, tracking loss may occur, and the system currently relies on re-initialization rather than active relocalization. Nevertheless, the adaptive keyframe selection and the interval adjustment mechanism provide some robustness to moderate motion changes and scene complexity. Integrating lightweight loop closure and relocalization modules into the 3DGS representation is a promising direction for future work, aiming to extend the framework to full SLAM capabilities while preserving its real-time performance.

## 5\. Conclusion

In this paper, We presented ViMGS-SLAM, a real-time monocular VSLAM framework that synergistically integrates a Multi-scale Vision Transformer (MViT) with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). The MViT generates metric-scale depth priors through a hierarchical pyramid architecture, which are used to initialize and constrain an explicit 3D Gaussian scene representation. A synchronous tracking–mapping pipeline, equipped with adaptive keyframe selection and anisotropic regularization, jointly optimizes camera poses and Gaussian parameters. Extensive evaluations on the TUM RGB-D and Replica benchmarks demonstrate that ViMGS-SLAM significantly outperforms existing methods. On TUM fr3, it reduces the absolute trajectory error by 46.0% (from 0.0437 m to 0.0236 m) compared to MonoGS. On Replica, it achieves state-of-the-art novel-view synthesis with PSNR 39.6 dB, SSIM 0.976, and LPIPS 0.042. The system operates at 2.7 FPS end-to-end in monocular mode, while the differentiable renderer alone reaches $1130$ FPS, confirming its efficiency. Ablation studies validate the contribution of each component: the MViT depth prior, the photometric and depth losses, and the isotropic regularization term.

Despite these advances, ViMGS-SLAM currently lacks explicit loop closure, relocalization, and long-horizon drift correction, which are common to most 3DGS-based SLAM systems. As shown in, trajectory error gradually accumulates over long sequences, highlighting the need for global optimization. Future work will focus on integrating lightweight loop closure modules and exploring asynchronous execution to further improve scalability and robustness. We believe this work provides a solid foundation for developing more sophisticated and efficient 3DGS-based SLAM systems in the future.

Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (VSLAM) serves as a fundamental technology for constructing dense metric 3D maps of complex environments, thereby providing the critical geometric foundation for autonomous systems. Among its various implementations, monocular VSLAM presents a compelling yet challenging paradigm due to its reliance on a single camera, which introduces significant difficulties in recovering 3D geometry from 2D images. Self-supervised monocular depth estimation, which bypasses the requirement for expensive ground-truth depth by framing depth prediction as a view synthesis problem, has garnered substantial research interest. This paradigm leverages the principle of photometric consistency, jointly optimizing depth and pose estimation networks to minimize reprojection error across multiple views of a scene. Despite notable progress, self-supervised monocular depth estimation remains fundamentally constrained by several geometric challenges: scale ambiguity, unreliable depth cues in textureless regions, and blurred object boundaries resulting from insufficient geometric priors. To address these limitations, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a promising direction, offering global self-attention mechanisms that more effectively model long-range dependencies and integrate contextual information compared to conventional convolutional networks. Consequently, while ViTs enhance representational capacity, their potential for achieving robust, metric-aware geometric understanding remains constrained under the self-supervised paradigm.

## 2\. Related work

### 2.1. Monocular depth estimation

Based on deep learning, monocular depth estimation methods can be principally categorized into supervised, semi-supervised, and self-supervised paradigms. 1) Supervised methods establish direct image-to-depth mappings using pixel-wise ground truth annotations. Representative works include DCNNs and VGG-based frameworks, which achieve high accuracy but require expensive depth annotations, limiting their scalability in real-world applications. 2) Semi-supervised methods mitigate data constraints through consistency regularization and pseudo-labeling techniques. While reducing annotation dependency, these approaches remain susceptible to error propagation and domain shift between labeled and unlabeled data distributions,. 3) Self-supervised methods have gained prominence by reformulating depth estimation as a view synthesis problem. These approaches leverage geometric constraints from stereo or monocular sequences, minimizing photometric discrepancies through image warping operations. Recent advances include Dyna-MSDepth, which employs multi-scale spatial-semantic interactions to generate stable depth estimates in dynamic environments. Despite progress, self-supervised monocular depth estimation faces persistent challenges: (1) dynamic object motion violating static scene assumptions, (2) inherent scale ambiguity in monocular setups, and (3) escalating training complexity with multi-modal data. Future research should therefore prioritize the development of efficient multi-scale architectures that achieve robust scale consistency.

### 2.2. Self-supervised depth estimation

Self-supervised depth estimation has garnered substantial research attention due to its label-free training paradigm. Nevertheless, two major challenges still persist. First, self-supervised frameworks inherently lack metric scale recovery, which necessitates auxiliary sensor data (e.g., inertial measurements or camera intrinsics) to establish physical scale Refs.. However, inconsistencies in camera configurations across training datasets hinder the development of unified scale-aware models. While mainstream datasets emphasize structured scenes, they remain limited in representing dynamic objects and extreme illumination variations. Addressing scale invariance and offset robustness across heterogeneous datasets thus constitutes a critical research direction for self-supervised paradigms. Second, domain gaps between datasets significantly degrade model generalizability. Recent advances in zero-shot cross-dataset generalization aim to bridge this gap by enabling metric-compatible predictions without dataset-specific fine-tuning. Pioneering works such as MegaDepth and MiDaS have demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale multi-dataset training, while OmniData introduced systematic strategies for cross-domain adaptation. Emerging architectures leveraging Transformers, ViT and diffusion models exhibit enhanced capacity for cross-dataset feature disentanglement, achieving notable progress in scale-consistent depth transfer. Despite these advancements, limitations persist in three aspects: 1) suboptimal accuracy under occlusions and textureless regions, 2) sensitivity to camera parameter variations, 3) compromised inference efficiency in multi-scale frameworks. To address these challenges, we propose a hierarchical multi-scale ViT architecture that synergistically integrates local texture details and global contextual cues through three distinct resolution pathways.

### 2.3. 3DGS-based SLAM

Within VSLAM research, reconstructing metrically consistent 3D environments from monocular image sequences remains a fundamental challenge. Extensive studies have demonstrated that elliptical primitives outperform conventional point or line features in geometric localization accuracy due to their enhanced spatial constraints,. A seminal advancement was made by Zwicker et al., who introduced Elliptical Weighted Average (EWA) Splatting - a sophisticated rendering framework employing anisotropic Gaussian kernels. Their work proved that EWA Splatting achieves alias-free reconstruction while preserving sharp discontinuities in volumetric data, effectively handling non-spherical scattering effects. This methodology demonstrates exceptional versatility across regular, curvilinear, and unstructured volumetric datasets, establishing a unified framework for multi-modal data processing. Subsequent ellipse-based methods advanced dynamic scene understanding, yet practical adoption in SLAM remains constrained by reliance on precise calibration, multi-view constraints, and sparse observation sensitivity. The advent of 3DGS has revolutionized this paradigm by enabling unified optimization of geometry and appearance through differentiable volumetric rendering, significantly advancing SLAM reconstruction efficiency and visual quality. Despite these advancements, monocular 3DGS SLAM implementations remain under-explored, particularly regarding scale consistency in depth estimation and computational complexity management. Our work addresses these limitations through three key innovations: 1) A hybrid depth prediction network combining monocular geometric priors with multi-scale Gaussian uncertainty modeling, 2) A ViT-gaussian fusion framework for photorealistic novel view synthesis, and 3) Adaptive algorithms enabling real-time performance on both RGB and RGB-D streams.

### 3.1. System overview

For monocular inputs, the MViT module (Section ) generates dense depth priors through multi-scale attention mechanisms, ensuring geometrically consistent depth estimation without compromising temporal coherence. The system supports both RGB-D and RGB-only sensor configurations. Following depth initialization or RGB-D data acquisition, the 3DGS representation module (Section ) transforms input data into anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives via differentiable reparameterization. These primitives explicitly encode scene geometry (position, covariance) and photometric properties, forming a continuous volumetric representation suitable for differentiable rendering. Concurrently, the camera tracking module (Section 3.4.1) estimates camera poses in real time by iteratively minimizing a composite loss function that incorporates both L1 distance and structural similarity (SSIM). This formulation effectively balances subpixel intensity alignment with structural consistency, ensuring stable and accurate pose regression. The tracking output is then processed by the keyframing module (Section 3.4.2), which employs an adaptive selection strategy based on relative pose displacement and keyframe overlap density, complemented by an adaptive interval adjustment mechanism that dynamically modulates insertion frequency according to tracking loss. This strategy systematically prunes redundant keyframes to optimize computational efficiency while preserving the topological integrity of the constructed map. Within the mapping module (Section 3.4.3), ViMGS-SLAM dynamically regulates the integration of 3D Gaussian primitives based on real-time pose estimations from the front-end tracking module, achieving iterative optimization of scene geometry parameters under isotropic regularization to preserve spatial–temporal consistency and suppress artifacts. Through this tightly coupled architecture, ViMGS-SLAM maintains sub-centimeter geometric accuracy and real-time performance, even in challenging scenarios involving textureless surfaces, occlusions, or repeated patterns.

### 3.2. Multi-scale Vision Transformers (MViT)

To enable geometrically accurate 3D reconstruction in visual SLAM and overcome the limitations of self-supervised monocular depth estimation, we propose a novel architecture based on MViT. This design integrates multi-scale visual analysis with geometric constraints in feature learning, addressing key challenges in self-supervised paradigms such as scale ambiguity, sensitivity to textureless regions, and blurred object boundaries.

As shown in, the model comprises two tightly coupled components. First, the multi-scale input embedding stage employs linear projection and scale-adaptive positional encoding to map multi-resolution image patches into a unified feature space. All input images —whether from TUM RGB-D (originally 640 × 480) or other databases—are padded to a square and resized to 640 × 640. The MViT then constructs a three-level pyramid by further resizing to 640 × 640, 320 × 320, and 160 × 160. At each scale, the image is divided into non-overlapping patches using a convolutional layer with kernel size 16 × 16 and stride 16. Each patch is processed by a weight-shared Vision Transformer encoder, producing feature maps of size 24 × 24 per patch. These patch-level features are then merged into a full-resolution feature map at the corresponding pyramid level using a sliding-window merging strategy with 25% overlap, ensuring seamless reconstruction across patch boundaries. Subsequent feature projection employs 1 × 1 convolutions (stride 1) for channel alignment, spatial refinement uses 3 × 3 convolutions (stride 1), and upsampling stages rely on 2 × 2 transposed convolutions (stride 2) to progressively recover resolution. All convolutional layers in the feature fusion blocks and the output head use the ReLU activation function. This efficient multi-scale design enables real-time operation under fast vehicle motion by keeping inference latency low. Second, the multi-scale feature fusion stage implements an attention-based feature pyramid network that ensures consistency between local geometry and global context through cross-scale interaction and spatial–semantic alignment, enhancing depth discontinuity preservation and geometry inference in weakly-textured regions.

The multi-scale feature input and embedding module serves as the core encoding architecture of our MViT. It decomposes the input image into multi-scale patches $\mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ at scales $s \in \mathcal{S}$, applies linear projection via a learnable embedding matrix $\mathbf{E}$ together with scale-aware positional encodings $\mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$, and extracts hierarchical representations using a pre-trained dual-branch Transformer encoder $\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)}$. Each patch is processed by the weight-shared encoder, producing a feature map of size 24 × 24. These patch-level features are subsequently merged and upsampled to construct a multi-scale feature pyramid comprising five levels with spatial resolutions of 320 × 320, 160 × 160, 80 × 80, 40 × 40, and 20 × 20, and channel dimensions of 256, 512, 1024, 1024, and 1024, respectively. Inspired by BeiT, the dual-branch encoder is designed to capture both local and global visual patterns: a local branch focuses on fine-grained details, while a global branch models long-range semantic dependencies. Through the fusion function $\Phi$, which performs cross-scale concatenation and spatial-semantic alignment, these two streams collectively form a unified multi-scale feature pyramid. This design preserves both local geometric structures and global contextual understanding, establishing an essential foundation for accurate geometric reconstruction in subsequent stages. The unified multi-scale feature representation $\mathcal{F}$ can be written as: (1) $\mathcal{F} = \Phi \left(\left(\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)} \left(\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} + \mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}\right)\right)_{s \in \mathcal{S}}\right)$ where the embedded representation for an input image at scale $s$ is given by $\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} + \mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$. The token sequence $\mathbf{x}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ is linearly projected via $\mathbf{E}$ and augmented with scale-dependent trainable positional encodings $\mathbf{P}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$. A Transformer encoder $\mathcal{H}^{\left(\right. L \left.\right)} \left(\right. \cdot \left.\right)$ with $L$ layers then processes the embedded features, extracting rich contextual representations through multi-head self-attention and multilayer perceptron modules. The fusion function $\Phi \left(\cdot_{s \in \mathcal{S}}\right)$ integrates encoded features across all scales in $\mathcal{S}$ via cross-scale concatenation and spatial–semantic alignment, yielding a unified multi-scale feature representation $\mathcal{F}$.

The dense feature upsampling and depth reconstruction module, built upon a DPT-based decoder, accurately maps multi-scale features to high-resolution depth estimates. After merging and upsampling, the obtained feature pyramid with five levels—320 × 320, 160 × 160, 80 × 80, 40 × 40, and 20 × 20—is first fused with global contextual features and then progressively upsampled via $2 \times$ transposed convolutions, ultimately restoring the original input resolution of 640 × 640. Inter-scale misalignment is mitigated through explicit spatial alignment operations. The aligned features from all scales are then aggregated. The resulting unified multi-scale representation is condensed into a single-channel inverse depth map using a 1 × 1 convolutional layer. This design effectively integrates multi-scale cues in a coarse-to-fine manner, restoring high-resolution geometry while preserving pixel-level geometric continuity and sharp boundaries—forming the core of our sub-pixel geometric fidelity. The predicted inverse depth map $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1}$ can be written as: (2) $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1} = \mathbf{W}_{d} \circ \text{Upsample}_{1 : 1} \left(\right. \underset{s \in \mathcal{S}}{\oplus} \text{Align} \left(\right. \text{Upsample}_{t \leftarrow s} \left(\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} \oplus \mathbf{F}_{g}\right) \left.\right) \left.\right)$ where $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{- 1}$ is the predicted inverse depth map, generated by applying a pointwise convolutional kernel $\mathbf{W}_{d}$ to the output of a final upsampling operation $\text{Upsample}_{1 : 1}$, which restores the aggregated multi-scale features to the original input resolution. This aggregation $\oplus_{s \in \mathcal{S}}$ combines features from all scales $s$ in the set $\mathcal{S}$, where each scale’s contribution is processed by a spatial alignment function $\text{Align}$ that corrects feature misalignments. The input to the alignment function is the upsampled result of $\text{Upsample}_{t \leftarrow s}$, which rescales the fused features from scale $s$ to a common target scale $t$. This fusion $\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)} \oplus \mathbf{F}_{g}$ combines scale-specific encoder features $\mathbf{F}^{\left(\right. s \left.\right)}$ with global contextual features $\mathbf{F}_{g}$ via a skip connection.

The MViT is trained as a zero-shot metric depth estimator on a diverse mixture of real-world and synthetic datasets using a combination of supervised losses, including mean absolute error (MAE) on metric ground truth and scale-and-shift-invariant losses on non-metric data. This training enables the network to directly predict absolute-scale inverse depth maps from a single RGB image without requiring camera intrinsics or metadata, providing a robust metric prior for subsequent SLAM modules.

### 3.3. 3DGS representation

The 3DGS representation provides a unified parametric form for scene modeling that bridges continuous 3D geometry and discrete rendering through a collection of anisotropic Gaussians. The collection of anisotropic 3D Gaussians $\mathcal{G} = \left{\right. G_{i} \left.\right}_{i = 1}^{N}$ is defined as: (3) $\mathcal{G} = \left(G_{i} \left(\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}_{i} , \mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} , O_{i}\right)\right)_{i = 1}^{N} , \mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} = \mathbf{R}_{i} \text{diag} \left(\right. \mathbf{S}_{i} \left.\right) \mathbf{R}_{i}^{\top}$ Where $\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the Gaussian’s center, $\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is its covariance matrix, and $O_{i} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the opacity. The covariance is decomposed into a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{i} \in SO \left(\right. 3 \left.\right)$ and scaling factors $\mathbf{S}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, ensuring positive semi-definiteness and enabling stable gradient-based optimization. This parametric form enables efficient differentiable optimization and direct splat-based rasterization.

During rendering, each 3D Gaussian is projected to 2D via a local affine approximation and composited using alpha blending. The projected 2D covariance $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}$ is derived from the Jacobian of the projective transform, while the final pixel color is obtained by depth-ordered blending of all contributing Gaussians based on their projected densities and opacities. (4) $\mathbf{C} \left(\right. p \left.\right) = \underset{i \in \mathcal{N}}{\sum} \mathbf{c}_{i} \alpha_{i} \prod_{j = 1}^{i - 1} \left(\right. 1 - \alpha_{j} \left.\right) , \alpha_{i} = O_{i} exp \left(- \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{\top} \overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}^{- 1} \mathbf{\Delta}_{i}\right)$ Where $\mathbf{\Delta}_{i} = p - \overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}}_{i}$ is the pixel offset, $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\mu}}}_{i}$ and $\overset{̃}{\mathbf{\mathit{\Sigma}}}_{i}$ are the projected mean and covariance, $\alpha_{i}$ is the effective density, and $\mathbf{c}_{i}$ is the Gaussian’s color. This formulation enables real-time, high-quality rendering through differentiable splatting and ordered blending.

### 3.4. Framework

Our framework comprises three tightly integrated components. Tracking estimates camera poses through photometric and depth residuals, keyframing maintains a representative subset of views for efficient mapping, and mapping optimizes the 3D Gaussian representation with geometric constraints.

These modules form a closed-loop system where tracking relies on keyframes for pose initialization, mapping utilizes keyframe observations for scene reconstruction, and keyframe selection is guided by both tracking stability and mapping quality requirements.

(1) Tracking. Camera pose estimation is initialized via orthographic projection and optimized through a combined photometric and depth loss. The hybrid loss leverages both L1 and SSIM metrics for color alignment and incorporates depth residuals when available, with adaptive weighting between modalities. Gradient computation utilizes CUDA-based rasterization for efficiency, while Gaussian parameters remain fixed to ensure mapping stability. The tracking loss is formulated as: (5) $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}} = \lambda_{c} \left(\mathcal{L}_{1} \left(\right. \hat{I} , I \left.\right) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}} \left(\right. \hat{I} , I \left.\right)\right) \bigodot M_{\text{rgb}} + \lambda_{d} \mathcal{L}_{1} \left(\right. \hat{D} , D \left.\right) \bigodot M_{\text{depth}}$ Where $\hat{I}$ and $\hat{D}$ represent the rendered image and depth, $I$ and $D$ are ground-truth measurements, $M_{\text{rgb}}$ and $M_{\text{depth}}$ indicate valid pixels, and $\bigodot$ is element-wise multiplication. In monocular operation, where depth measurements are unavailable, we set $\lambda_{d} = 0$ and rely solely on photometric loss for pose estimation. However, to anchor scene geometry to a metric scale during mapping, the MViT-predicted depth is treated as pseudo ground truth $D$, enforcing geometric consistency through the depth term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$. Optimization runs for $\geq$ 50 iterations per frame using Adam to ensure convergence without map updates.

2) Keyframing. Keyframe management employs a sliding window strategy to balance reconstruction quality and computational load. It consists of two complementary components: a geometry-based selection criterion and an adaptive interval adjustment mechanism. New keyframes are inserted based on overlap and displacement thresholds relative to recent frames, while outdated ones are removed when overlap diminishes. This ensures sufficient viewpoint coverage while maintaining real-time operation. The keyframe selection criteria are defined as: (6) $\text{Insert if}: \text{IoU} \left(\right. \mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n} \left.\right) < \theta \text{or} \frac{L_{m n}}{\hat{D}_{m}} > \xi ,$  $\text{Remove if}: \text{OC} \left(\right. \mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n} \left.\right) < \theta - \xi$ where $\mathcal{G}_{m} , \mathcal{G}_{n}$ denote Gaussian representations, $\text{IoU}$ and $\text{OC}$ measure geometric overlap, $L_{m n}$ is relative displacement, and $\hat{D}_{m}$ is median depth. Thresholds $\theta , \xi$ are dataset-specific (e.g., TUM: $\theta = 0 . 85 , \xi = 0 . 05$; Replica: $\theta = 0 . 95 , \xi = 0 . 04$). Monocular setups use static thresholds, which may limit adaptability.

To address this limitation, we introduce an adaptive keyframe interval adjustment mechanism that dynamically modulates the insertion frequency based on scene complexity. The keyframe interval $\Delta_{\text{kf}}$ is updated according to the tracking loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}}$ as follows: (7) $\Delta_{\text{kf}} = \begin{bmatrix} max \left(\right. 2 , \Delta_{\text{default}} - 2 \left.\right) , & \text{if} \mathcal{L}_{\text{track}} > \omega \\ min \left(\right. 3 , \Delta_{\text{default}} + 1 \left.\right) , & \text{otherwise} \end{bmatrix}$ where $\Delta_{\text{default}}$ is the default interval and $\omega$ is a predefined loss threshold. The tracking loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{track}}$ serves as a proxy for scene complexity and motion uncertainty. When the loss exceeds $\omega$, indicating challenging conditions, the interval is reduced (subject to a minimum of 2) to insert keyframes more frequently, thereby strengthening geometric constraints and mitigating primitive distortion. Conversely, when the loss is low, the interval is increased (up to a maximum of 3) to reduce computational redundancy. This adaptive strategy enhances geometric consistency and textural authenticity while maintaining a favorable balance between resource usage and reconstruction quality.

(3) Mapping. Scene reconstruction optimizes Gaussian parameters with explicit regularization to constrain anisotropic scaling in poorly observed regions. The mapping loss combines photometric, depth, and isotropic regularization terms, preventing artifact formation while preserving geometric fidelity under limited viewpoints. The mapping objective is defined as: (8) $\mathcal{L}_{\text{map}} = \lambda_{c} \mathcal{L}_{\text{color}} + \lambda_{d} \mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}} + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \left(\parallel \mathbf{S}_{h} - \overset{̄}{S}_{h} \mathbf{1} \parallel\right)_{2}$ Where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{color}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$ mirror the tracking losses, $\mathbf{S}_{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the Gaussian scaling vector, $\overset{̄}{S}_{h}$ is its mean, and $\mathbf{1}$ is a vector of ones. The regularization term penalizes deviation from isotropic scaling, suppressing excessive elongation. Weights satisfy $\lambda_{c} + \lambda_{d} = 1$ (with $\lambda_{d} = 0$ if depth is unavailable) and $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ controls regularization strength. Based on empirical validation, we set $\lambda_{c} = 0 . 8$, $\lambda_{d} = 0 . 2$ when depth is available, and $\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 0 . 05$.

Specifically, when operating in monocular mode, the depth prior $D$ in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{depth}}$ is obtained from the MViT forward pass on the current keyframe. The L1 penalty between the rendered depth $\hat{D}$ and this prior anchors the optimization to a metric scale and effectively mitigates scale drift over time.

This performance gap underscores ViMGS-SLAM’s capability to mitigate error propagation through temporal feature aggregation and occlusion-aware attention.

Overall, the integration of the MViT depth prior with the explicit 3DGS representation enables ViMGS-SLAM to achieve robust metric-scale reconstruction and accurate camera tracking. As demonstrated in, the MViT generates high-fidelity depth maps with sharp boundaries, serving as reliable geometric anchors throughout the pipeline. This leads to a substantial reduction in scale drift, as evidenced by the absolute trajectory error on the TUM fr3 sequence (), where ViMGS-SLAM outperforms MonoGS by 46.0%. Furthermore,, shows that ViMGS-SLAM consistently delivers superior rendering quality across all TUM and Replica sequences, achieving higher PSNR and SSIM scores and lower LPIPS values than the compared baselines. Collectively, these results validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework in enhancing both geometric consistency and photometric fidelity.

The RMSE ATE increases by 2.41 cm compared to the full model, underscoring the critical role of photometric constraints in maintaining localization accuracy. This degradation occurs because photometric loss provides essential visual feedback through pixel-intensity alignment between current and reference frames, which is particularly crucial for handling illumination variations and textureless regions where depth cues are unreliable.

(2) Ablation of Depth Loss. Removing the depth loss results in an RMSE ATE increase of 1.8×, highlighting its importance for geometric accuracy. The depth loss supplies metric-scale constraints by leveraging scene geometry, which directly mitigates the scale ambiguity inherent in monocular SLAM. Nevertheless, the system retains functional tracking and mapping capabilities, indicating that photometric and regularization terms can partially compensate for the absence of depth supervision in static environments.

(3) Ablation of Isotropic Regularization. The removal of isotropic regularization leads to a performance degradation comparable to that of depth loss ablation, with ATE increasing from 1.48 cm to 2.53 cm. This confirms its essential role in suppressing excessive Gaussian elongation and controlling error propagation. As discussed in Section, the regularization term $\left(\parallel \mathbf{S}_{h} - \overset{̄}{S}_{h} \mathbf{1} \parallel\right)_{2}$ penalizes deviation from isotropic scaling, encouraging more compact and geometrically consistent primitives. ### 4.7. Runtime results analysis

summarizes the end-to-end runtime performance of ViMGS-SLAM on the TUM fr2/desk sequence. The system achieves an average frame rate of 2.7 FPS when processing monocular RGB streams and 2.3 FPS with RGB-D input. These figures correspond to the complete synchronous execution of the pipeline per frame: tracking, keyframe management, and mapping are performed sequentially, guaranteeing that the map is consistently updated with the most recent observations. The mapping module averages 19.6 optimization iterations per keyframe in monocular mode and 17.7 iterations when depth data is available; this iteration count directly influences the overall frame rate, as each iteration involves gradient computation and parameter updates for the growing set of Gaussian primitives. The observed trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and throughput reflects the inherent cost of dense geometry optimization in a synchronous design, while the rendering stage alone sustains over 1100 FPS, confirming the efficiency of the differentiable Gaussian splatting backend. To better understand the computational characteristics, we examine the rendering subsystem separately. As shown in, the color refinement stage—which involves only forward rendering of the 3D Gaussian representation—achieves an average of 1130.2 FPS, with a peak of 1440.8 FPS. This high throughput demonstrates the exceptional efficiency of the differentiable Gaussian splatting pipeline. However, the overall frame rate remains lower because each tracking step requires multiple rendering passes to compute the photometric loss and gradients, and the mapping step iteratively updates Gaussian parameters. The computational cost of mapping scales with the number of Gaussians, which in our experiments grows to approximately 95,000 primitives on the TUM fr2 sequence. Consequently, the observed trade-off between frame rate and reconstruction accuracy reflects the inherent cost of dense geometry optimization. While the current synchronous design prioritizes geometric consistency, future work could explore asynchronous execution or adaptive iteration budgets to further increase throughput while maintaining quality.

While ViMGS-SLAM achieves accurate dense reconstruction and real-time rendering, its current design does not incorporate explicit loop closure, relocalization after tracking failure, or long-horizon drift correction—modules that are essential for a complete SLAM system. This is not a limitation specific to our approach; indeed, most existing 3DGS-based SLAM methods also focus on front-end tracking and dense mapping, with global optimization and loop detection remaining open research challenges in this emerging paradigm. Consequently, our system behaves as an odometry-only framework, and the absolute trajectory error gradually accumulates over extended sequences, as observed in on the TUM fr2/desk dataset (2965 frames). In challenging scenarios such as rapid motion or severe occlusion, tracking loss may occur, and the system currently relies on re-initialization rather than active relocalization. Nevertheless, the adaptive keyframe selection and the interval adjustment mechanism provide some robustness to moderate motion changes and scene complexity. Integrating lightweight loop closure and relocalization modules into the 3DGS representation is a promising direction for future work, aiming to extend the framework to full SLAM capabilities while preserving its real-time performance.

## 5\. Conclusion

In this paper, We presented ViMGS-SLAM, a real-time monocular VSLAM framework that synergistically integrates a Multi-scale Vision Transformer (MViT) with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). The MViT generates metric-scale depth priors through a hierarchical pyramid architecture, which are used to initialize and constrain an explicit 3D Gaussian scene representation. A synchronous tracking–mapping pipeline, equipped with adaptive keyframe selection and anisotropic regularization, jointly optimizes camera poses and Gaussian parameters. Extensive evaluations on the TUM RGB-D and Replica benchmarks demonstrate that ViMGS-SLAM significantly outperforms existing methods. On TUM fr3, it reduces the absolute trajectory error by 46.0% (from 0.0437 m to 0.0236 m) compared to MonoGS. On Replica, it achieves state-of-the-art novel-view synthesis with PSNR 39.6 dB, SSIM 0.976, and LPIPS 0.042. The system operates at 2.7 FPS end-to-end in monocular mode, while the differentiable renderer alone reaches $1130$ FPS, confirming its efficiency. Ablation studies validate the contribution of each component: the MViT depth prior, the photometric and depth losses, and the isotropic regularization term.

Despite these advances, ViMGS-SLAM currently lacks explicit loop closure, relocalization, and long-horizon drift correction, which are common to most 3DGS-based SLAM systems. As shown in, trajectory error gradually accumulates over long sequences, highlighting the need for global optimization. Future work will focus on integrating lightweight loop closure modules and exploring asynchronous execution to further improve scalability and robustness. We believe this work provides a solid foundation for developing more sophisticated and efficient 3DGS-based SLAM systems in the future.
